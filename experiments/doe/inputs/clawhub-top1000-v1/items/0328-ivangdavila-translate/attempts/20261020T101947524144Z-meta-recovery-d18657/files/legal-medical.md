# Regulated Work — Legal, Medical, Financial, Certified

Text where a mistranslation has a consequence beyond embarrassment: a claim dismissed, a dose miscalculated, a filing rejected, a device recalled. The craft changes shape here — accuracy outranks readability, ambiguity is preserved rather than resolved, and the procedure around the translation matters as much as the words.

**Before starting regulated work for a client**, read `artifacts/certification-*.md` and the pair's glossary if `## Boxes` names them. Requirements are per authority and per jurisdiction, and they were established once by asking.

## Red Flags

Anything in this table suspends the normal workflow. These are observable properties of the job, not judgment calls.

| Signal | What it means | Action |
|---|---|---|
| The text will be filed with a court, registry, immigration office, or university | A certification requirement almost certainly applies | Ask the receiving authority what it accepts, before translating (`Certification`) |
| The source is a scan, a photo, or a partially illegible copy | A certified translation cannot be produced from an unverifiable original | Request the original or a certified copy; mark illegible passages, never guess |
| The text carries a dosage, a concentration, a rate, or a device setting | A numeric error is a patient-safety event | Two-pass numeric verification, and no unit conversion without written instruction |
| It is an informed-consent form, a patient questionnaire, or a validated instrument | An ethics committee, not the client, defines the required process | Forward-backward translation with reconciliation; ask which process the committee mandates |
| The document is a contract that will exist in two languages | The translation may be legally operative text | Establish which language prevails before starting (`Contracts`) |
| A deadline is set by a court or a regulator | A late filing is worse than an imperfect one, and often unfixable | Confirm the deadline and the format in writing before accepting |
| The client asks for machine translation of any of the above | The output cannot carry the required assurance | Decline the MT route and explain the exposure in one line (`Machine Translation Here`) |
| The source contains a claim about efficacy, safety, or financial return | Approved wording lists usually govern | Route to the client's regulatory or legal reviewer with the back-translation attached |
| You are asked to "clean up" or "improve" the source | Improving regulated text changes its legal effect | Translate what is there; raise the defect separately as a note |
| The content is a medical, legal, or financial *decision* rather than a text | Outside translation entirely | Say so plainly and route the user to a qualified professional in that field |

## Certification

There is no international certification. **The receiving authority decides what it accepts**, and the same document can need three different treatments in three countries. Ask them first; the answer is usually a single sentence and it saves the whole job.

| Jurisdiction | Usual mechanism |
|---|---|
| United States, United Kingdom, Australia | A signed statement of accuracy from the translator or agency; no government licence exists; some bodies additionally want it notarized |
| Spain | *Traductor jurado* appointed by the foreign ministry; stamp and signature on every page |
| Germany, Austria | Court-sworn translator (*beeidigt* / *vereidigt*), appointed regionally |
| France | *Traducteur assermenté* on a court of appeal's list |
| Italy | *Asseverazione*: the translator swears the translation before a court clerk or notary |
| Poland, Czechia, Slovakia | Sworn translator on a ministry register, with a numbered repertory entry |
| Netherlands | Translator registered in the Rbtv |
| Mexico, Argentina, Brazil | *Perito traductor* / *tradutor público juramentado*, registered locally |
| China, Japan | Usually agency translation plus a company seal, then notarization |

Two things are commonly confused with certification and are separate steps: **notarization** (a notary confirms the identity of the signer, not the quality of the translation) and an **apostille** (Hague Convention authentication of the *original document* or of the notary's signature, obtained from the issuing country's designated authority — never from the translator).

## How a Certified Translation Looks

- Complete: nothing omitted, including headers, footers, stamps, marginal notes and text on the reverse.
- Non-text elements described in brackets where they appear: `[Round seal: Ministry of Justice, Madrid]`, `[Illegible handwritten signature]`, `[Illegible]`. Never omit and never invent.
- Layout follows the original closely enough that a reader can hold them side by side, with the original's page breaks marked.
- Names, numbers, dates and reference numbers transcribed exactly, in the original script where the authority allows it, with a transliteration where it does not (`numbers-and-names.md`).
- No commentary, no correction of errors in the original; an obvious error is reproduced and flagged in a translator's note, if notes are permitted.
- The statement of accuracy carries the translator's name, qualification, signature, date, and the language pair, and is attached to a copy of the source that has been marked as the source translated.

## Legal Translation Principles

- **Translate the text, not what it should have said.** Ambiguity in the source is reproduced in the target; resolving it is drafting, which is not the translator's role and can change the outcome of a dispute.
- **Terminology follows the legal system, not the language.** Argentine, Mexican and Spanish law all use Spanish and name things differently. Establish the target jurisdiction before the target language.
- Concepts without an equivalent — *consideration*, *trust*, *equity*, *estoppel* in common law; *cause*, *usufructo*, *Treuhand* in civil law — are handled by a functional equivalent with the original term in brackets on first use, or by keeping the original term with a gloss. Choose one policy per document and record it in the pair's glossary.
- **False friends between systems are the classic trap**: a *society*, a *company*, an *executive*, a *magistrate* and a *tribunal* do not name the same institution across jurisdictions.
- Defined terms are capitalized, listed, and rendered identically at every occurrence — a defined term translated two ways breaks the definition.
- Modal force is content: obligation, permission, prohibition and recommendation each map to fixed target-language forms. Rendering *shall* as a plain future tense weakens a covenant into a prediction.
- Clause, article and schedule numbering is never changed (`documents.md`).

## Contracts

- Find out whether the translation is **informative** (for understanding) or **operative** (a version the parties sign). Operative translation is bilingual drafting: it needs a lawyer qualified in the target jurisdiction, and the translator's role is to say so.
- Bilingual contracts normally carry a **prevailing-language clause**. Read it first: if the target is the prevailing version, every ambiguity you introduce becomes the contract.
- Party names, entity suffixes (`GmbH`, `S.L.`, `Ltd`), registered addresses, and registration numbers are transcribed, never translated (`numbers-and-names.md`).
- Governing law, jurisdiction, arbitration seat and institution names are kept in their official form, with a gloss if needed.
- Dates, amounts, interest rates, notice periods and cure periods get a dedicated verification pass. Currency is never converted (`numbers-and-names.md`).

## Clinical and Patient-Facing

- **Validated instruments** — patient-reported outcome questionnaires, scales, diaries — follow a prescribed process, typically: two independent forward translations, reconciliation, back-translation by a translator who has not seen the source, review against the source, cognitive debriefing with patients in the target country, and a documented report of every change. The instrument's owner licenses the process and often the translation itself; using an unlicensed version invalidates the data.
- **Informed consent forms** are approved by an ethics committee or IRB, must be comprehensible to a lay reader at the reading level the committee specifies, and usually require back-translation as evidence. Simplifying beyond that specification is as much a defect as writing above it.
- Protocols, investigator brochures and safety reports are internal documents where terminology consistency with the sponsor's existing glossary outranks elegance.
- Pharmacovigilance narratives carry dates, doses, timelines and outcomes that are the entire content: verify each one against the source separately from reading the prose.
- Patient-facing material in a health system is often subject to a plain-language requirement; translating a complex source into an equally complex target satisfies the letter and fails the purpose. Flag the mismatch to the client rather than deciding alone.

## Dosages and Numbers

The safety conventions in medication text exist because specific errors have killed people. Preserve them in the target:

- **A leading zero before a decimal point is mandatory** (`0.5 mg`, never `.5 mg`), and **a trailing zero is forbidden** (`1 mg`, never `1.0 mg`) — the classic ten-fold error is a decimal point lost or misread.
- Do not introduce abbreviations for units of measure or frequency that the target's safety guidance discourages; write units out where the source does.
- Never convert a dose, a concentration or a rate. If a conversion is requested in writing, give both values and the calculation.
- Verify every number twice: once in the flow of the sentence and once in a numbers-only pass over the whole document. The second pass finds what the first cannot.
- Decimal and grouping separators differ by locale (`numbers-and-names.md`) and a misread separator in a dose is exactly the failure to prevent — where the target's convention allows, prefer a form that cannot be misread.

## Devices and Labeling

- Medical device instructions for use must be supplied in the official language or languages of each market where the device is sold; in the EU this is a regulatory requirement, not a commercial choice, and it applies to labels, IFU and safety information.
- Symbols on labels are standardized and are not translated; the accompanying text is.
- Safety signal words (danger / warning / caution / notice) map to the target's standardized terms and required formatting (`documents.md`).
- Machinery, chemicals, toys and electrical goods carry their own language requirements per market, usually including safety data sheets and warnings. Ask which regime applies before quoting.
- A change to translated labeling generally requires the same review and approval path as the original. Never treat it as a copy edit.

## Financial and Regulatory Filings

- Prospectuses, key information documents and fund reporting have language requirements set by the market's regulator, and often a prescribed structure and length. The structure is part of the compliance, not a layout preference.
- Audit opinions, going-concern language and legal disclaimers have settled target-language wording. Find the standard text; composing it fresh is a defect.
- Figures, totals and footnote markers get the numbers-only verification pass described above (`documents.md`).
- Filing deadlines and submission formats are as binding as the text; confirm both in writing when accepting the work.

## Machine Translation Here

`mt_policy: never` applies to everything in this file, regardless of the configured value. Three reasons, all independent: the output cannot carry a certification statement; MT's characteristic failures are exactly the dangerous ones (dropped negation, altered modality, silent omission — `machine-translation.md`); and sending the source to a third-party engine usually breaches the confidentiality obligation attached to it.

Where a client insists on MT for cost, the honest answer is the exposure, in one line, and an offer of a different saving: a glossary, a translation memory, or a reduced scope (`jobs.md`).

## Confidentiality

- Regulated source documents contain personal data by default: patient records, salaries, identity numbers, medical history. **Nothing from them is stored** in `~/Clawic/data/` — not as a reference text, not as an example, not as a glossary row that quotes a patient.
- Terminology and procedure survive; content does not. A glossary row records the term, never the case it came from.
- Where a client's NDA specifies handling, deletion or a jurisdiction for the data, that overrides every default in this skill.

## What To Write Down

- **`artifacts/certification-<authority>.md`** the first time a receiving authority's requirement is established — who accepts what, in what form, with what attachment. Born as its own file with its `## Boxes` line, because the next request in that jurisdiction is otherwise researched from scratch.
- **Jurisdiction-specific term decisions** go to the pair's glossary with the legal system in the context column: the same source term takes different targets for Spain and Mexico, and both rows are correct.
- A **back-translation and reconciliation report** is an `artifacts/back-translation-<instrument>.md`; committees ask for it later.
- Deadlines and recurring regulatory reviews become rows in **`## Due`**; a certification that expires or a label review cycle belongs there, not in a memory of a conversation.
