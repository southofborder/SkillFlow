# Structure — Shape, Order, Openings, Endings, Titles

Most drafts that "read wrong" have correct sentences in the wrong order. Structure is diagnosable in five minutes with a reverse outline and unfixable by line editing (SKILL.md Rule 2).

**Contents:** [Reverse Outline: The Five-Minute Diagnosis](#reverse-outline-the-five-minute-diagnosis) · [Choosing the Shape](#choosing-the-shape) · [Ordering Within the Shape](#ordering-within-the-shape) · [Paragraphs](#paragraphs) · [Transitions](#transitions) · [Openings](#openings) · [Endings](#endings) · [Titles, Headlines and Subheads](#titles-headlines-and-subheads)

**Before restructuring a piece written for a publication, client or project**, read its `style-sheets/<context>.md` — required section order, length caps and mandatory blocks are structural constraints, and discovering them after the restructure means doing it twice.

## Reverse Outline: The Five-Minute Diagnosis

1. Write one sentence per paragraph saying what that paragraph **does** — argues, illustrates, concedes, transitions, defines.
2. Read the list alone, without the prose.
3. Three failures show up immediately:
   - **Two paragraphs with the same job** → merge, or cut the weaker one. This is where most of the cut target comes from (`revision.md`).
   - **A move that arrives before its prerequisite** → the reader had to hold an unexplained term. Move it, or move its definition up.
   - **A paragraph whose job is "transitions"** → delete it. A paragraph that only connects is a symptom of two sections that do not belong next to each other.
4. A list where every entry starts "explains" or "discusses" means the piece has no argument, only topics. That is a rewrite, not a reorder.

## Choosing the Shape

| Purpose | Shape | Order | Fails when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Someone must decide | **BLUF** — bottom line up front | Recommendation → why → alternatives rejected → next step | Used for an argument the reader will resist; they stop at the recommendation and never read the case |
| Someone must be persuaded of something they doubt | **Steelman-first** | Their position stated better than they state it → the fact it cannot explain → your account | Used on a friendly reader, who wonders why you are arguing |
| Executive briefing | **SCQA** (Minto) — situation, complication, question, answer | The shared situation → what changed → the question that raises → the answer | The "complication" is not actually new to the reader |
| News or announcement | **Inverted pyramid** | Most important fact first, detail in descending order | Used for narrative — the tension is spent in line one |
| Teaching a procedure | **Task order** | Prerequisites → steps in execution order → verification → failure modes | Steps are grouped by concept instead of by when they are done |
| A problem the reader lives with | **Problem-solution** | Their symptom in their words → cause → fix → what it costs | The symptom is stated in your vocabulary, not theirs |
| Personal or exploratory | **Question-attempts-turn** | The question → attempts that fail → the turn → what it means | The turn is announced ("but here's the twist") instead of earned |
| Comparison or decision | **Criteria-first** | The criteria that decide → each option against them → the pick | Options are described serially and the reader has to build the table |
| Anything else | Problem-solution — it is the shape most non-fiction readers arrive expecting | — | — |

One shape per piece. A memo that switches to narrative in the middle loses the reader who was skimming for the decision.

## Ordering Within the Shape

- **Strongest argument first, second-strongest last, weakest in the middle.** Readers remember the first and last positions; the middle absorbs the argument you cannot omit but would not lead with.
- **Concede before you are forced to.** An objection you raise yourself costs a sentence; the same objection occurring to the reader unprompted costs their trust for the rest of the piece.
- **Definitions arrive one paragraph before first use**, never in a glossary block at the top. A stack of definitions before the argument is the most common reason readers abandon technical writing.
- **Never make the reader hold more than one open loop.** "We will come back to this" is a debt; two simultaneous debts and comprehension collapses.
- **Examples go after the claim, not before.** An example before its claim reads as an anecdote and the reader spends it guessing what it is for.

## Paragraphs

- One job per paragraph, statable in the reverse outline. Two jobs means two paragraphs.
- Open with the sentence that states the job. A paragraph whose point arrives in its last sentence works in an essay and fails in anything skimmed.
- Length is a rendering decision: on a phone, four printed lines can occupy half a screen. For anything read on a phone, cap at three to four sentences; for print or a document, longer is fine.
- Vary length deliberately. A one-sentence paragraph is emphasis — which means it stops working when there are three of them on a page.
- The last sentence of a paragraph is the loudest position after the first. Do not spend it on a qualifier.

## Transitions

The best transition is correct order: paragraphs in the right sequence need almost no connective tissue.

| Connective | What it means | Misuse that signals a structural problem |
|---|---|---|
| `however`, `but` | The next claim contradicts the last | Used between two compatible claims to fake motion |
| `therefore`, `so` | The next claim follows from the last | Used where the logic is not actually entailment |
| `also`, `additionally` | Another item of the same kind | Three in a row means this should be a list |
| `first / second / third` | A counted sequence the reader can track | Announced count does not match the number delivered |
| `that said`, `of course` | A concession | Used to soften a claim you should either make or drop |

The repeated-noun link beats all of them: end a paragraph on a term and open the next with it. It is invisible and it never lies about the logical relationship.

## Openings

Written last, almost always. The opening's job is to make the second sentence unavoidable — nothing else.

| Lead | Use for | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Concrete scene | Essays, features, anything needing a reader to care before they understand | Self-indulgent length; two sentences is usually the budget |
| Number or fact | Analysis, business writing, anything where the surprise is the data | The number must be genuinely surprising, and sourced |
| Flat claim | Op-eds, memos, arguments | Reads arrogant if the claim is not actually contested |
| The reader's own symptom | Problem-solution pieces, product writing | Only works if phrased in their words, not yours |
| Question | Almost nothing | The reader answers it "no" and leaves; most writers' default and the weakest option |

Delete the first paragraph of the draft and check whether anything was lost. More often than not nothing was, and that paragraph was throat-clearing: context you needed to write your way in, not context the reader needs.

Banned openings: a dictionary definition, "in today's world", a rhetorical question, an apology for the length, a summary of what the piece will cover (the subheads already do that).

## Endings

An ending lands the consequence; it does not recap. A summary at the end of a short piece tells the reader the body failed.

| Ending | Use for | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| Consequence | Argument, analysis | What is now true, or what breaks if you ignore it |
| Next action | Memo, proposal, email | The single action, its owner, its date |
| Callback | Essay, personal writing | Return to the opening image, changed by what came between |
| The hard sentence | Anything with a real claim | The blunt version of the thesis, stated once, unhedged |
| Open question | Exploratory writing only | Genuinely open — a rhetorical question here is a cop-out |

Test: delete the final paragraph. If the piece still ends properly, that paragraph was a summary. If it now stops mid-air, the ending was doing its job.

## Titles, Headlines and Subheads

- A title makes a promise the piece must pay. Write it after the piece and check the promise against what got delivered.
- Specific beats clever. "How we cut the bill by 60% by deleting one gateway" outperforms "The hidden cost of complexity" because it names what the reader gets.
- Front-load the load-bearing noun: list views, search results, inbox previews and feed cards all truncate, and the truncation point varies by client and device. The first ~40 characters carry the decision.
- **Subheads state something.** "Why the first fix failed" is a subhead; "Background" is a label. A reader who reads only your subheads should get the argument — that is the actual test, and most drafts fail it.
- One subhead every 200-300 words in online prose; none at all in anything under 500 words.
- Sentence case or title case is house style, not correctness — take it from `style-sheets/<context>.md` or the `conventions` preference area.

**When a structural decision is made for a recurring context** — required section order, subhead cadence, a mandatory block — write it to that context's `style-sheets/<context>.md` in the same turn (`memory-template.md`). **When an outline outlives its piece** — a shape that will be reused for a series — it goes to `artifacts/<kebab-name>.md` with its `## Boxes` line.
